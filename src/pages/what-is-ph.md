## The basics

**Pulmonary hypertension (PH)** is a rare, complex and serious condition where the blood pressure in the lungs is higher than normal.

"Pulmonary" means relating to the lungs, and "hypertension" means high blood pressure. PH is very different from regular high blood pressure (systemic hypertension), which is measured with a cuff on your arm. PH affects the arteries in the lungs and the right side of the heart.

### How it affects the body

In a healthy body, the right side of the heart pumps blood through the pulmonary arteries into the lungs, where it picks up oxygen. The blood then returns to the left side of the heart to be pumped to the rest of the body.

In a person with PH, the walls of the pulmonary arteries become thick, stiff or blocked. This narrows the space for blood to flow through and increases the pressure. The right side of the heart has to work much harder to push blood through these narrowed arteries. Over time, this extra strain can cause the heart muscle to become weak and enlarged, leading to right heart failure.

## Common symptoms

In the early stages, symptoms of PH may be mild or hard to notice. As the condition progresses, symptoms become more severe. Because many symptoms are similar to other heart and lung conditions, PH can be difficult to diagnose.

- **Breathlessness (dyspnoea):** at first during exercise, and later even at rest.
- **Fatigue:** feeling constantly tired or exhausted.
- **Dizziness or fainting (syncope):** especially during physical activity.
- **Chest pain:** pressure or pain in the chest.
- **Swelling (oedema):** in the ankles, legs or abdomen, due to fluid build-up.
- **Palpitations:** a racing or pounding heartbeat.

## The five groups of PH

PH is grouped into five types based on the underlying cause. These are often called the WHO groups. Knowing your group matters, because it guides your treatment plan.

### Group 1: Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH)

PAH occurs when the arteries in the lungs become narrowed, thickened or stiff. It can be:

- **Idiopathic:** the cause is unknown. This is the most common type of PAH.
- **Heritable:** passed down through families.
- **Associated:** linked to other conditions such as connective tissue diseases (for example scleroderma), congenital heart disease, liver disease or HIV infection.
- **Drug- or toxin-induced:** associated with certain weight-loss medicines or illegal drug use.

### Group 2: PH due to left heart disease

This is the most common form of PH overall. It happens when the left side of the heart is not pumping or filling efficiently, for example because of heart valve disease or left ventricular failure. Pressure builds up in the left heart and backs up into the lungs.

### Group 3: PH due to lung disease or low oxygen

This group is caused by chronic lung conditions or long periods of low oxygen levels (hypoxia). Common causes include chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pulmonary fibrosis, interstitial lung disease and severe sleep apnoea.

### Group 4: Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH)

CTEPH occurs when old blood clots (pulmonary emboli) block or narrow the arteries in the lungs over a long period. Unlike other forms of PH, CTEPH can sometimes be cured or significantly improved with a specialised surgery called pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA), or a procedure called balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA).

### Group 5: PH with unclear or multiple causes

This group includes conditions that don't fit the other four groups, or where several factors contribute. Examples include certain blood disorders, sarcoidosis and some metabolic disorders.
